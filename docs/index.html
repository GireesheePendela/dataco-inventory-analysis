How much to trust the trapped-cash number. 33 of 118 SKUs (28%) have under 6 months of sales history, so their demand variability is estimated from their ABC class rather than measured. They hold 56% of the safety-stock capital and account for 77% of the trapped cash. The defensible claim is about $6K trapped on measured SKUs, up to about $28K if the estimated SKUs behave like their class.

Data and method

The data is the public DataCo Smart Supply Chain dataset (Kaggle, CC0): customer order lines, not warehouse records. It has no on-hand inventory column, so target stock is prescribed from demand behaviour rather than read from a stock level, and turnover is labelled “implied”.

  1. Aggregate (SQL). DuckDB rolls ~180K order rows into a SKU × month demand series plus per-SKU lead-time statistics.
  2. Demand profile (pandas). Each SKU's monthly series is zero-filled between its first and last sale, then reduced to mean and standard deviation. SKUs with under 6 months are flagged.
  3. Four layers. Implied turnover, ABC classification, EOQ, and safety stock / reorder point at 90/95/99% service.
  4. Mismatch analysis. An independent buffer-intensity metric finds where capital is mis-allocated.
  5. Report. One flat file, sku_metrics.csv, feeds the Power BI report.

Assumptions (not in the data): ordering cost S = $75 per order; holding cost = 25% of unit price per year; baseline service level 95% (z ≈ 1.65).

2. Implied turnover and velocity tiers

With no on-hand column, average inventory is derived from the sawtooth cycle-stock model (EOQ / 2). Because that assumes cost-optimal ordering, turns are a best-case benchmark that ranks relative velocity; it does not prove overstocking. The slow tier has a median of 45 days of supply and $9,693 of annual value per SKU, against 8 days and $317,582 for the fast tier.

6. Mismatch analysis: where is capital mis-allocated?

Implied turnover turns out to be an algebraic rescaling of ABC value (turns = √(2·H/S) × √annual value), so comparing the two would find a pattern the maths guarantees. I used an independent signal instead: buffer intensity, safety-stock dollars per dollar of annual throughput. It is driven by demand variability and lead time, not by value.

39 SKUs (all class B or C) are flagged over-buffered. The class-A SKUs are all efficiently buffered — their volume smooths their demand. Class B looks worst: 22 of 28 B-SKUs are in the heavy tier, though 17 of those 22 are estimate-driven.

8. Sensitivity to the service level

Safety stock scales linearly with z, so every class moves by the same percentage (−22% at 90%, +41% at 99%) — but the dollars are not evenly spread, because class A holds most of the buffer. Lifting only the 16 class-A SKUs to 99% costs $68,564 ($30,907 of it on the 8 class-A SKUs with estimated volatility), against $118,178 to lift the whole catalogue.

Recommendations

  1. Adopt EOQ ordering, starting with class A — the 16 A-SKUs drive most of the $145K a year available from right-sizing order quantities.
  2. Use a class-differentiated service policy — class A at 99%, B and C at 95%. It costs $69K of extra buffer against $118K for the whole catalogue, and nothing drops below the 95% floor.
  3. Go after trapped cash in over-buffered B/C SKUs through demand smoothing and shorter supplier lead times, not service cuts. Start with the $6K on SKUs with measured volatility (22 SKUs); collect more history on the other 17 before acting on the remaining $21K.
  4. Validate the 33 short-history SKUs before acting on their reorder points.

Confidence and limitations

  • 28% of SKUs have estimated volatility — 56% of safety-stock capital, and 20 of the 28 class-B SKUs.
  • The EOQ saving is against a hypothetical baseline, not actual purchase orders.
  • No on-hand inventory — turnover is implied and ranks velocity only.
  • Only demand variability is buffered. Lead times are short and stable (0–6 days).
  • Ordering cost and holding-cost rate are assumptions; the dollar figures scale with them.
